# Chiromancer

(a character-and-fate creator)

To run, open a build folder or workspace in a local server, such as VSCode's "Go Live" feature, or visit <https://wileywiggins.com/p5/chiromancer/>

---

## Research Statement

The character creation screen is a recurring feature in computer role-playing games (CRPG's), inheriting from a lineage of tabletop roleplaying games and wargames.

Games involving random number generation that emulate analog games of chance depend on actors with quantifiable features in order to model asymmetrical combat.

How do these sorts of quantified characters connect to pre-modern ideas of fate and character?

In digital computers, randomly generated numbers can be unpredictable but they are never truly random, as computation is deterministic.

In a digital game, the intervention of a human player steers that deterministic result, depending on the level of freedom they are allowed within the rules of the game world.

The mechanics of belief can be theological, philosophical, scientific, a priori, rote.

Byzantine rules for cementing belief may obscure relatively crude assumptions about the world couched within them.

Is the body a marker of fate or a diverting obstacle in its flow?

## Conclusion

What was interesting about this project to me, in retrospect, was my confrontation with control, randomness and determination in a game as a comment on metaphysical ideas about control, randomness and determination in life and in imagination of life. Journaling within the project's versioning system in the spirit of [Materializing Design Method](https://materializing.design) helped me consiously work through that and also allows for later citation in my games studies writing.

> I wasn't always sure I "got it" but mostly found grounding in this question "How do these sorts of quantified characters connect to pre-modern ideas of fate and character?" which I think is a nice one. Did you answer it? ([Barr 2026](/journal/2026-10-26-feedback.md))

There are different overlapping domains that I deal with independently in this prototype and in the prototype that follows (Phrenologist). This prototype deals with ideas of character and fate and Phrenologist deals with character and a quantified self/body.

This prototype connects with ideas I had while writing [The (New) Tower of Babel](https://games.ucla.edu/text-archive/wileywiggins/), which has its own larger constellation of interconnected subjects, which indlude ideas of computation, divination, and language.

Digital games, especially the sorts of computer role playing games and roguelikes that use dice-roll type "randomness" as a decider of encounters like combat, inherit systems from tabletop roleplaying games and wargames that can at times look very much like real world systems for quantifying, evaluating and identifying human beings. These systems, no matter how objective they present themselves, inherit from a long lineage of racist and eugenicist ideologies, which over the course of history we have seen borrow all of the mechanics of belief aquisition that Peirce listed in his famous essay (1877). I visit some of this history in a section of my [aforementioned writing](https://games.ucla.edu/text-archive/wileywiggins/the-degenerate-utopia/). In the context of computer game history it feels unfair to make this connection between the convenience of computability, especially in a genre of game that began life alongside the computer itself, and the history of quantifying individuals, but I'd argue that there is an association worth thinking about, as moribund ideas are carried forward in these sorts of containers, and those ideas are worth talking about when we study games.

In this particular prototype I'm also interested in much murkier ideas about fate. Walter Benjamin has a particularly challenging essay on these topics, which I pulled from in [another section](https://games.ucla.edu/text-archive/wileywiggins/fate-and-language/) of my Tower of Babel writing—

> For Benjamin, the reading of mantic symbols such as signs and portents belongs to the ossified order of myth. Fate is the realm of guilt, debt, and predetermination—the religious domain in which human beings are powerless to interfere in the workings of a cosmic order. Susan Buck-Morss writes in The Dialectics of Seeing:

> Within myth, the passage of time takes the form of predetermination. The course of events is said to be predestined by the gods, written in the stars, spoken by oracles, or inscribed in sacred texts. Strictly speaking, myth and history are incompatible. The former dictates that because human beings are powerless to interfere in the workings of fate, nothing truly new can happen, while the concept of history implies the possibility of human influence upon events (1989). [^1]

One of the default forms of software-as-art I see over and over in creative coding and art communities is an enchantment of the computer, or the LLM, as a divination system, incorporating ritual elements, sometimes borrowing legitimacy from the cultural identity of the artist. I don't wish to make broad value judgments about these sorts of projects— those judgments could be misconstrued as a judment about identities, or an unfair assumption of artistic intent. I can also appreciate divination as a form of pre-computational writing with a ruleset, and as a literary form. I do want to bring ideas about fate and free will back into the conversation, however. And that has led me to make several of my own 'divination' software sketches. This is one of these. In making this sketch I read a number of 19th century palmistry handbooks. They were explicitly racist, absurd, and they borrowed their authority from every available source— the Bible, the Kabbalah, the Vedas, the ancient Greeks, in order to make unqualified declarations about the connection between lines on the human hand, essential character traits as an unchanging and predetermined rather than socially, materially, medically arrived-at qualities that change over time. Through those traits, as sort of predestined guilt-markers, the fate of the individual is cast and can be seen in the hand.

---

[^1]: This may have been misleadingly reductive on my part, as *Fate and Character* does a lot of complex work to recover the idea of divination for metaphysics, if not for real life claims of some pre-existing supernatural connection between signs and fate.

> alongside Benjamin's emphatic rejection of occult magic and its divinatory impulses there is an equally emphatic investment in precisely the magical traditions and divinatory practices we can trace from antiquity throughout the early modern period and even into Romanticism. For this reason it seems more accurate to claim, not that Benjamin is committed to the disenchantment of magic reading in his work, but that he is intent on clearing space for re-approaching and reasserting its truths. Not, then, to refute magic reading and assign it to some long lost past but, in however "weak" a form, to redeem it and its future.
